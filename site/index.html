As a kid I pictured things I couldn’t build. Now I can build almost anything I picture.

Who I am

I’ve been fascinated by technology and digital experiences since I was a kid in the 90s. If a room had a computer or knobs to turn, that’s where you’d find me. So naturally, I started DJing with a friend. When I stumbled onto Beatport’s website back in 2011, I was hooked. I wanted to build things like that, immediately. That pull never left. FPV drones, festival apps, a car that explains itself: I get obsessed every time digital meets the real world. My method hasn’t changed much: impatient, working like crazy until it works, getting up while everyone is still asleep.

Being a developer in 2026 and beyond

I think the point of leveraging AI as a developer is not creating more things. I strongly believe it should be used to create experiences that weren’t possible before. The bottleneck of using AI to create digital experiences seems to be the review process, not only by the creator, but by the end user too. Feedback cycles are still bound by human constraints.

What I do best

Give me a vague idea and I’ll make it come alive. Woov started as one line: “find my friends at a festival”. I imagined what it could look and feel like, then built it until it held up on a real festival field.

What makes me valuable is imagination, the technical and design kind: seeing how an idea could work before anyone has drawn it. Then I’m a lever. I dive in headfirst, use everything I can get my hands on, and cut through the politics to get it done.

I’m at my best building interactive things that let people do something, together with other creative people. Not managing a team for you, but making the idea real alongside you.

Featured work

Rave to Save

Desperados wanted dancing to save nightlife: every 1,000 steps raised €1 for nightlife. My job was to turn a dancing crowd into live data. Sensor wristbands and phones counted every step and sent it to my server. It combined a whole dancefloor into live stats on the club’s big screens. The harder the crowd danced, the more it raised.

Woov

The brief was one line: find your friends at a festival. I created branded festival maps that showed your friends moving across the field, live. The hard part was keeping those live locations stable. It worked in testing and broke once the festival filled up. A lot of late nights later, it worked really well. We hit 5,000 users at launch, at Soenda.

Testdrive that explains the car

A car brand wanted to turn a test drive into something more. My part was using the car’s surroundings, so a voice agent could explain its features at exactly the right moment. Drive past a supermarket, and the car tells you about its trunk size.

Strength training app

I took six months off to finish my own product: a strength training app. I built something that worked but was a dime a dozen. So I made it AI-first, with a coach built in. Then other projects started pulling at my focus. And AI made it hard to say “this is good enough”, because adding something new is so easy. I’ve been fighting drift ever since. It’s the clearest lesson I have for why AI should make one thing better, not more things.

Legacy rebuild

I rebuilt the website and portal of a Dutch development fund on a modern stack, with AI agents doing what used to take a team. The hard part wasn’t the technology. It was uncovering years of decisions buried in the old system. Now it’s a foundation the fund can keep building on.
